Research interests

Inspired by Sartre’s idea that “Man is nothing else but that which he makes of himself,” I often reflect on who I am becoming through my choices and actions, and want to break free!

As a researcher studying organizations, this curiosity leads me to consider when we see organizations as actors capable of pursuing purposes and acting on their own behalf, and how these perceptions shape the experiences and actions of people within and around them. My research, mianly based on surveys and experiments, approaches these questions from two complementary perspectives:

  • Organization theory

    I examine how organizations come to be understood as social actors with identities, purposes, and capacities to act (organizational actorhood), and how these perceptions shape stakeholders’ judgments and responses.

  • Organizational behavior & Social psychology

    I investigate when and how perceptions of an organization as a coherent, unified entity (organizational entitativity) enable or constrain individual agency, particularly among employees.

Thesis chapters

Actorhood & creativity

When organizational actorhood helps and hurts creativity: The contingent role of learning goal orientation

Seeing an organization as a purposeful actor may help some employees think more creatively while constraining others. Why does the same perception have such different effects? This project examines how employees’ learning goal orientation shapes the answer.

Abstract

Organizations differ in the extent to which they are perceived as unified, self-determining actors. Although prior research shows that such perceptions of organizational actorhood shape how external stakeholders evaluate organizations, little is known about how they influence the behavior of organizational members. Moreover, the prevailing view that organizational actorhood constrains individual agency is theoretically underspecified and does not yield clear predictions about employee outcomes. We develop a norm-based perspective to explain how organizational actorhood shapes employee creative performance. We argue that higher organizational actorhood strengthens perceived organizational norms, which in turn structure employees’ cognitive processes. Critically, these effects on cognitive processes depend on employees’ learning goal orientation. For employees low in learning goal orientation, stronger norms provide cognitive scaffolding that enhances cognitive flexibility and, consequently, creative performance. In contrast, for employees high in learning goal orientation, stronger norms constrain exploratory thinking, reducing cognitive flexibility and undermining creative performance. We test these predictions using a field survey and two experiments. Across studies, we find that organizational actorhood has opposing effects on creative performance depending on employees’ learning goal orientation, and that these effects are mediated by cognitive flexibility. By demonstrating that organizational actorhood can both facilitate and inhibit employee creativity, our findings advance research on organizational actorhood and creativity, and provide a more nuanced understanding of how organizational context shapes employee cognition and behavior.

Perception (dis)agreement & entitativity

Seeing through shared expectations: How organizational entitativity shapes competence image consistency within stakeholder groups

AOM 2026 · Best Paper Proceedings

Why do people looking at the same organization sometimes form similar impressions and sometimes see very different things? I examine how perceived organizational unity shapes whether stakeholders agree about an organization’s competence.

Abstract

Organizational image research has primarily examined how positively organizations are evaluated, paying comparatively little attention to whether members of the same stakeholder group form similar impressions of the same organization. Yet such image consistency is often implicitly assumed and may shape how stakeholders collectively understand and respond to organizations. We examine when and why competence images converge or diverge within stakeholder groups. Drawing on research on organizational entitativity and expectancy violation, we argue that people hold category-level expectations that organizations are coherent social actors. When an organization is perceived as highly entitative, these expectations are confirmed, allowing stakeholders to rely on shared interpretive frameworks when forming competence impressions. In contrast, when an organization appears low in entitativity, these expectations are violated, prompting more exploratory and idiosyncratic information processing and leading stakeholders to rely on different cues. As a result, competence images become less consistent across observers. We find support for these predictions across one field survey and two experiments. These findings introduce image consistency as a theoretically meaningful property of organizational images, identify perceived entitativity as a key determinant of whether stakeholders form shared impressions of organizations, and highlight a novel implication of expectancy violation theory for the convergence or divergence of social judgments across observers.

Vision communication & change support

When organizational vision inspires change supportive behavior: The role of organizational entitativity

Can a shared vision inspire action when employees do not see a unified organization behind it? This project examines how organizational entitativity shapes inspiration and support for the changes needed to realize that vision.

More about this project

Organizational vision refers to a desired future state of an organization. Drawing on the transformational leadership tradition, communicated visions are commonly assumed to inspire and motivate employees. This research examines whether perceived organizational entitativity—the extent to which employees perceive their organization as a coherent and unified entity—shapes how inspiring they find vision communication. It further examines whether this inspiration is associated with the effectiveness of vision communication, as reflected in employees’ change-supportive behavior: their efforts to embrace and contribute to the changes needed to realize the organization’s communicated future.
